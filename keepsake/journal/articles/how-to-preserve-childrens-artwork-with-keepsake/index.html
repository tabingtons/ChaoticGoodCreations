How To Preserve Children’s Artwork With Keepsake

A simple way to capture drawings, preserve the story behind them, and build a collection you’ll want to revisit.

By Harry Brown · September 2026

Keepsake gives you a simple way to preserve the artwork that comes home, along with the details that make each piece meaningful.

Whether you want to save a few favourites or create a complete digital record of your child’s artwork, the process is straightforward: capture the piece, add the story behind it, and organise it so you can find it again later.

The real value comes from preserving the artwork while the context is still fresh and giving yourself a way to return to those memories over time.

That makes the process less about storing paper and more about keeping a record of your child’s creativity as it changes.

Start with the artwork you want to preserve

If there are already months or years of drawings in drawers and boxes, you can begin wherever feels right.

You might work through a backlog, capture the latest piece that came home from school, or start with an artwork that has a particularly memorable story attached to it.

Keepsake can help you preserve all kinds of children’s artwork, from everyday drawings and paintings to school projects, handmade cards and larger pieces that have spent time on the fridge or wall.

You can save as much or as little as you like.

The important thing is that each piece can be kept together with the details that help you remember when it was made and what it meant at the time.

Capture the artwork in Keepsake

Once you have an artwork ready, open Keepsake and start a new keepsake using the artwork capture option.

The goal at this stage is simple: create a clear digital version of the piece without making the process feel like a project.

Lay the artwork flat in good, even light if possible. Avoid strong shadows across the page, and try to keep the camera directly above the artwork so the shape remains natural.

You do not need a studio setup. A kitchen table near a window is often enough.

What matters is that the artwork can still be appreciated later without the photograph itself getting in the way.

Once the artwork is captured, the important part begins.

Why this is different from simply taking a photo

Taking a photograph is easy. The difficulty is what happens to it afterwards.

A photo of a drawing can sit safely in your camera roll for years and still become almost impossible to find among screenshots, holidays, pets, school events and every other image captured along the way.

Even when you do find it, the photograph may no longer tell you very much.

You might remember that your child made it, but not how old they were. You may have forgotten what they called it, what was happening when they made it, or the long explanation they gave you when they first showed it to you.

Keepsake is built around preserving that surrounding context, because the story is often what makes the artwork worth returning to.

The image preserves what they made.

The details preserve why it mattered.

Add the story while you still remember it

The best time to add context is usually when the artwork is still sitting in front of you.

Keepsake gives you room to record the details that might otherwise disappear over time. You do not need to fill in everything for every piece, but a few small details can make an enormous difference later.

Give the keepsake a title that will still make sense years from now. Add who made it, the date if you know it, and your child’s age at the time.

Then use the notes or reflection area for anything the artwork itself cannot explain.

Perhaps your child gave the picture a name. Maybe they told you what was happening in it, even though the explanation bears very little resemblance to what appears on the page. Perhaps it came home after their first week at school, or there was something funny about the moment they handed it to you.

Write that part down.

It does not need to be polished. In many cases, one sentence is enough.

Age four. She said this was our family camping on Mars and was very clear that the dog had his own spaceship.

That detail may eventually become more valuable than the image itself.

Use extra photos to capture more of the memory

Not every artwork memory is contained within the paper itself.

Sometimes the interesting part is how it was displayed, how large it was, or what was happening around it.

A huge painting taped to the wall may be worth preserving in context before it comes down. A school project might include several pieces. A drawing could have something written on the back that you want to remember alongside the front.

Keepsake lets you add extra photos to capture those details.

You can include additional views of the artwork, photos of it displayed in your home, or images of related pieces that belong to the same memory.

These extra photos help create a fuller record of the artwork and the moment around it.

Give the artwork a place in your Memory Boxes

Once the keepsake itself is complete, you can add it to one or more Memory Boxes.

Memory Boxes are useful because the same piece can belong to more than one part of a family story.

A drawing might sit in a box for one child, but also belong in a collection for their first year of school. A favourite handmade card might belong in both a family box and a birthday collection.

You can create Memory Boxes around the groups you would naturally want to browse later.

That might be:

  • Emma
  • Year 1
  • Favourite Artwork
  • Kindy Years
  • Things That Made Us Laugh

The labels can be practical or personal. The point is simply to make future browsing feel natural.

Decide what happens to the original

Digitally preserving something does not automatically mean the physical version needs to go.

Some pieces should stay.

A first drawing may deserve a frame. A favourite piece might belong in a portfolio. A particularly meaningful card or artwork may be something you still want to hold in your hands years later.

Keepsake does not need to replace that.

Digital and physical preservation can work together.

The difference is that once a piece has been captured properly and the story has been saved alongside it, you have more flexibility in deciding what happens next.

The original can stay on the fridge for another month. It can go into a physical memory box. Or, if you no longer need to keep the paper itself, you can let it go knowing that the image, the age, the date and the story are still preserved together.

That can make the decision feel very different.

You are no longer choosing between keeping the object forever and losing the memory completely.

Build the habit around moments or backlogs

Keepsake is easy to use whenever you are ready to preserve artwork.

A new piece arrives. It spends some time on display. You ask your child about it. Then you capture it and add the story while the details are still familiar.

You can also use Keepsake to work through artwork you have already collected.

If you have a backlog, you can preserve it gradually or capture several pieces whenever you have time. There is no requirement to finish everything at once, and there is no limit to how much of your child’s artwork you can keep.

The important thing is that the artwork and its context are stored together, whether you capture it on the day it comes home or years after it was made.

The point is not only to save the artwork

Preservation is only half of what Keepsake is designed to do.

The other half is revisiting.

A drawing becomes more interesting with time because the child who made it keeps changing. The handwriting becomes neater. The favourite subjects change. The way they draw people, houses, animals or themselves develops almost without notice.

That is when an old piece can suddenly carry more weight than it did when you first saved it.

Keepsake is designed to make those memories easier to return to rather than allowing them to disappear into another digital archive.

A piece you captured a year ago can become a reminder of a particular age. A favourite artwork can resurface after you have forgotten about it. A Memory Box can become something worth opening because it brings together the artwork and the stories that surround it.

Over time, those individual keepsakes begin to form something larger: a visual record of the drawings, phases and small moments that might otherwise have become scattered across boxes and camera rolls.

That is where the difference between storing something and preserving it properly becomes clearer.

The goal is not only to make sure the artwork survives.

It is to make sure you can still find your way back to the memory.

Your family memories remain private

Children’s artwork often contains more personal information than it first appears to.

There may be a name written in the corner, a school reference, handwriting, family details or a note about something meaningful that happened at the time.

That is why privacy is built into the way Keepsake works.

Keepsake does not require you to create a Keepsake account, and your memories are stored using your own iCloud rather than being uploaded to a separate Keepsake-owned content service.

There is no social feed, no advertising model built around your family memories, and the content you preserve is not something Keepsake needs access to in order for the app to work.

The artwork and the stories stay where they belong: with you.

A collection worth coming back to

The best way to use Keepsake for children’s artwork is to preserve it in a way that keeps both the image and its story together.

Capture the artwork while the context is still fresh, add the details you want to remember, and organise it in a way that makes sense for your family.

That might be ten pieces in a year. It might be fifty. It might be every drawing your child brings home.

The number does not matter nearly as much as having a collection that still feels like your child when you return to it.

A first.

A favourite.

A strange phase.

A masterpiece according to nobody except the person who made it and the person who kept it.

Those are the things Keepsake is for.
