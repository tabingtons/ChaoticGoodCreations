What To Do With a Whole Year of School Artwork

It comes home on the last day of term, all at once. Here’s how to get through it in one evening, with your child choosing what stays.

By Harry Brown · December 2026

It comes home on the last Friday of term, in a supermarket bag that’s been folded over twice. Paintings stuck together at the corners. A self-portrait with very long arms. Something made of egg cartons that has lost a bit on the way.

It goes on the kitchen bench. It’s still there on Monday.

Nobody wants to deal with it, because dealing with it feels like deciding which bits of a whole year matter. That’s a lot to ask of a Tuesday night.

The best thing to do with a year of school artwork is to sort it once, the week it comes home: let your child choose a handful to keep, write one line on the back of each (their name, their age and what they said it was), photograph the ones with a story, and let the rest go. It takes about ten minutes. The year still fits in one box.

How much of a year’s school art should you keep?

Less than it feels like. A whole school year can be a hundred pieces or more, and most of them were practice: the same rainbow, the same worksheet, the same handprint turkey as the rest of the class.

A gentle rule of thumb is as many as fit in one box, and a box smaller than you’d think. Somewhere between five and fifteen pieces is plenty for a year.

The ones worth keeping usually have one of these:

  • Their writing. Their name spelled their way, a caption, a speech bubble.
  • Their idea. Something nobody asked them to draw.
  • A size or an age you’ll want to remember. A handprint, a self-portrait, a first go at writing the whole family’s names.
  • A story. If you already know what they’ll say about it, that’s a keeper.

Let them choose the keepers

This is the part that makes it quick, and it’s the advice parents give each other most: tip the bag out on the floor, show them the box, and let them pick what fits.

Children are often much less sentimental about their own art than we are. They’ll choose things you wouldn’t, and walk past things you’d have kept. That’s fine. Their choice is part of the record. In ten years, the fact that they picked the dinosaur over the self-portrait tells you something about who they were at six.

If there’s one they skip that you can’t let go of, keep it too. You’re allowed one.

What helps:

  • Do it together, not after bedtime. It’s quicker with them, and you get the stories for free.
  • Have the box there before you start. A limit they can see works better than a number.
  • Say “goodbye”, not “rubbish”. Some kids like to hold each one before it goes. Let them.

Write the story on the back, tonight

The art will keep. What it was won’t.

Right now, your child can tell you that the green shape is “Dad mowing the lawn, but the lawn is angry”. By next December they won’t remember, and neither will you.

For each keeper, write on the back, in pencil, small:

  • their name and how old they are
  • the date, or at least the year and the class
  • what they said it was, in their words

That’s it. One line. If they’ve got more to say, write more, or record them saying it.

What about the huge ones and the 3D ones?

Every bag has a few that won’t fit in any box: the long collage of the whole class, the papier-mâché planet, the cardboard castle with a working drawbridge.

  • Big flat pieces: photograph them flat on the floor in daylight, then keep a corner (the bit with their name on it) or let them go.
  • 3D pieces: photograph them from a couple of sides, or save them in 3D so you can turn them around later. Keep the very best one if there’s room on a shelf for a while.
  • Group work: a photo with your child pointing to “their bit” says more than the piece itself.

There’s more on clay, LEGO and cardboard in How To Keep Your Kids’ 3D Creations.

One box per school year

The simplest system for years of school art is one box per year, labelled with the year and the class: “Year 1, 2026”. Shoe boxes or document boxes both work. Flat is better than folded.

When the next bag comes home, the next box comes out. No sorting the old years again, no deciding whether 2026 needs more room. Each year gets the same amount of space, and that’s what keeps it manageable over thirteen years of school.

If you’ve already got a few years in a cupboard, The Best Way To Organise Years of Children’s Drawings walks through sorting the backlog.

How Keepsake can help

Keepsake is for the story as much as the picture. For a year of school art, you can:

  1. Make a Memory Box for the year, like “Kindergarten” or “Year 1”, and put each keeper in it.
  2. Add the story to each piece: how old they were, what it was, what they said about it.
  3. Add a voice note, so you keep how they said it. One voice note per keepsake is free; listening back is always free.
  4. Save the 3D ones in 3D. On an iPhone with a LiDAR scanner, the clay and cardboard pieces can be saved so you can turn them with a finger later.

The ones you let go can live in Keepsake with their story, so letting go of the paper doesn’t mean losing the moment. Your keepsakes stay in your own iCloud, not on a public platform.

Next December

But there’s a box on the shelf now, with “Year 1” on the side. Inside, a dinosaur your child chose, and on the back, in your handwriting: age six, “he’s not scary, he’s just big”.
