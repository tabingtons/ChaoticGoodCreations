How To Preserve Handmade Cards, Letters and Sentimental Objects

Simple ways to preserve the things that are too meaningful to throw away, from handwritten cards to small objects and family keepsakes.

By Harry Brown · September 2026 · Updated November 2026

The card has more glue than glitter. On the front, a Christmas tree, if you already know it’s a tree. Inside, TO MUM AND DAD in letters of three different sizes, and a row of kisses that ran out of room and carried on round the corner.

It’s been in the kitchen drawer since last December, under the takeaway menus. You don’t need it. You also can’t throw it out.

Keep the cards and letters with handwriting you’d know anywhere, store them flat in one dry box out of the sun, photograph every side, and write down who made each one and when while you still remember.

That’s most of it. The rest of this page is the detail: which kinds of keepsakes need which kind of care, what to do with the Christmas cards your kids bring home, and when a photo is enough.

Why sentimental objects are so difficult to declutter

Most things in a house answer easy questions. Do you use it? Does it still work? Is it worth the cupboard space?

Sentimental objects don’t answer any of them.

A birthday card can sit untouched in a drawer for ten years and still be impossible to throw out. A gift from a grandparent might be something you’d never have bought yourself, which is almost the point. It’s kept for who it came from, not for what it does.

That’s why most decluttering advice doesn’t land here. “When did you last use it?” isn’t much help when the thing in your hand is the last birthday card someone wrote to you, or a slightly crooked craft your child made at five.

The attachment is real, and it’s allowed to be.

Keeping everything has its own cost, though. When the drawer fills with cards, letters, crafts and small things, the ones that matter most end up underneath the rest, and the box turns into something you’d rather not open. A few things kept well usually beat a lot of things kept anyhow.

Different keepsakes need different kinds of care

A letter and a small ceramic bird carry their meaning in different places. The letter is words, handwriting and paper. The bird is shape and weight, a chip on one wing, and the memory of being handed it.

Knowing where the meaning sits makes the choice easier: keep the original, keep a photo, or keep both.

Handmade cards

A handmade card is usually several things at once. A drawing on the front, a message inside, a name on the back, and the small accidents that make it itself. A sticker on upside down. A fingerprint in the paint. “Happy Brithday”.

To preserve handmade cards well, photograph every side, not just the cover. The inside is often the part you’ll want most later.

Keep the original too when the paper, the texture or the way it was put together is part of it. A pop-up that doesn’t quite pop is worth holding.

Handwritten letters

With letters, the words are the heart of it, but the handwriting is the person. The slope of the letters. How hard the pen pressed. The way someone always wrote a “g”. You’d know it across a room.

That’s why people keep old letters long after they’ve stopped rereading them. You’re not only reading a message. You’re looking at something their hand made.

For the important ones, keep every page, and the envelope too. A postmark, an old address or a note on the flap can end up meaning more than you’d expect.

Children’s crafts

Children’s crafts don’t fit in folders. They’re bulky, they’re fragile, and they’re made of things that were never meant to last: cardboard that warps, glue that lets go, pipe cleaners, paint that flakes, and 3D projects that drop a piece every time you move them.

Keep the favourites, and the ones with a story attached. For the rest, a few photos from different angles hold the shape far better than one shot of the front, especially when part of the memory is how it was made, or what your child said it was. (It’s a dog. It has always been a dog.)

Small gifts and meaningful objects

Some keepsakes were never meant to be keepsakes. A keyring. A shell. A ticket stub, a small toy, a ring that doesn’t fit any more. They matter because of who gave them to you, or where you were.

To anyone else they look like nothing much. Without the story, there’s no clue why they were kept. That’s why the story matters as much as the thing.

What to do with the Christmas cards your kids make

December brings a lot of them home. The one from school, the one from kindy, the one made at the kitchen table and handed over still wet. Almost everything written about them is about making them. Much less is about what happens after.

A few things that help:

  • Keep the ones with their writing inside. The picture on the front often looks a lot like last year’s. “TO NANA LOVE FROM” in this year’s handwriting doesn’t.
  • Write the year and their age on the back, in pencil. Pencil is gentle on paper and won’t bleed through to the front.
  • Store them with the decorations. A flat envelope or folder in the Christmas box means they come out every December with everything else. Plenty of families already do this with special cards, and it suits the handmade ones best of all.
  • Put the glitter in a sleeve. Loose glitter and cotton-wool snow go everywhere. A clear sleeve keeps the card in one piece and the rest of the box clean.
  • Photograph the front and the inside before they go up. Cards on display get bent, faded and leaned on. A photo on the first day keeps it as it was when it was handed to you.

Cards your kids give to grandparents count too. If a grandparent has kept theirs, ask for a quick photo of the inside. It’s one of the few places you’ll see what your child chose to write to someone else.

For the paper chains and salt-dough stars that come home with them, there’s a separate guide to the Christmas crafts kids make.

When the original is worth keeping

Some things are the memory. A letter from someone important. A child’s first handmade card. A small gift that belonged to a grandparent. Anything where the texture, the wear or the mistakes are part of who it’s about.

Paper has weight. Ink changes colour. A fold shows how a letter was carried. A handmade thing holds fingerprints and uneven edges that make it unmistakably itself.

If the object gives you something a photo can’t, keep it. Not everything needs that, and that’s fine.

When a photograph or digital copy is enough

For plenty of keepsakes, the picture and the story are what matter. A huge card that won’t fit in any box. A craft that’s already coming apart. Three nearly identical paintings of the same cat.

Photographing or scanning these keeps what they looked like and leaves you free to decide what happens to the original. For flat things like letters and cards, a clear photo is usually enough, as long as every side and every page is in it.

Things with a shape want a bit more: the front, the back, the sides and any odd details. Some are worth capturing in 3D, because the meaning is in the shape and not on any one surface.

You’re aiming for enough that, years from now, you can still see why it mattered.

A few simple ways to protect the originals you keep

None of this needs museum-level care. A little goes a long way.

  • Paper likes dry, steady and dark. Damp cupboards, garages, attics and anywhere that swings between hot and cold are hardest on cards and letters. So is strong sunlight.
  • Use acid-free sleeves, folders or boxes for the most precious paper. It’s less about building an archive and more about keeping things clean, flat and away from anything that might stain them.
  • One labelled box beats a clever system. You’ll actually keep using it, and you won’t have to dig through everything to find one card.
  • Give objects a bit of room. Keep heavy things off delicate ones, keep everything dry, and wrap fragile pieces in tissue so they don’t rattle around loose.

Not perfect. Just a fair chance of lasting.

Preserve the whole object, not just the obvious side

The easiest thing to miss is the side you didn’t think of. The card photographed from the front, with the message inside left out. The letter saved without its envelope. The craft shot from one angle, with a name scrawled on the bottom.

Those are often the bits you’ll go looking for later. Before you put something away, turn it over.

Handwriting most of all. A name, a date, a two-word message. A child’s writing changes from one year to the next. An older relative’s becomes something you’d know before you’ve read a word of it. Keeping the writing is keeping a bit of the person.

The story is what turns an object into a keepsake

A photo shows what a card looks like. It doesn’t say that it was the year she insisted on doing the envelope herself, or that the reindeer has five legs on purpose.

That part lives outside the object, and a few details are enough to hold it:

  • Who made it, or gave it to you?
  • When, and what for?
  • How old were they?
  • Why did you keep it?
  • Is there anything about it that wouldn’t be obvious from the picture?

It doesn’t need to be a catalogue. A sentence or two does it.

Giving sentimental things a life beyond the drawer

Some things you’ll keep because holding the original matters. Others matter less than the person, the handwriting or the moment attached to them. Both are good ways to keep something.

A letter might live in a box for the rest of your life. A handmade card might stay in the Christmas box for ten years and in your phone after that. A fragile craft might last best as a few photos and the name your child gave it. A small object might need a few angles before you’re ready to let the original go.

Sometimes the meaning is in the thing. Sometimes it’s in the handwriting, or the person who made it, or the ordinary moment it was handed to you. Usually it’s a bit of all of them.
